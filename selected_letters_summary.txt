20. [LETTER_064] To ROGER ZELAZNYJ 

_December 1, 1972_ 

## Dear Roger, 

Such a nice letter from you, and congratulations about Devin. Tessa and I have hopes in that direction, but it will be a while before we can be sure. Meanwhile, we are happy for you and Judy. Maybe he will inherit his father’s writing ability. Speaking of writing ability—I dreamed the other night that I was up on a stage making a speech of acceptance for an award, but I was not too clear in my mind what the award was for. Harlan Ellison was the only person in the audi­ ence and he was laughing. I finally was told what the award was for: I had set the Bible into rhymed couplets. I got out of there fast. Fast dissolve. 

...In the beginning God separated the water from the land, And exclaimed, “My, isn’t that grand.” Etc. Which is a distinct loss compared with Martin Luther’s, “And God saw that it good was.” Which I always liked. 

The civilized quality of your letter makes me feel like a barbarian, and my news, which I had promised to tell you, the ravings of a semi-barbaric churl. I arrived in Canada carrying a knife. There’s an example right there, and I got the knife by stealing it from the girl I was going with in San Rafael, whom I ditched, saying I’d be back in California in four days (I’ve never seen her again). In Van­ couver I had an affair with the wife of the reporter who covered the Convention for the _Province._ I also offered to beat him up. I had an affair, too, with a com­ puter programmer who, when I first visited her apartment, asked me two ques­ tions: (one) “Want to smoke dope?” and (two) “Want to go to bed?” I said yes to both. The dope was terrible. On the other hand, I had a really tender relationship with a gentle little hip­ pie chick named Jamis, there in Vancouver, who was the reason for my suicide attempt. I still sort of love her. One day we were on CKLG FM live for almost two hours; the next day I heard her mention to some other people that she was leaving Vancouver in a month. The next day I tried to do myself in. The next day I was mopping bathrooms and scrubbing pans all day at X-Kalay, which is like Sinanon [sic

65. [LETTER_207] To MARCEL THAONJ _September 5, 1973_ 

## Mr. Thaon, 

Sir, I know about your work on my behalf, and I will devote all energy possi­ ble to answering your astute and yet kind letter. I will simply labor at it until I have got it right. One reason I say this is because my wife Tessa and I just a month ago had a new baby, and we are both quite tired caring for him, but none­ theless I will keep my promise to devote myself to answering you. After all, what you have done for me, and the arousal of interest in my work in France, is beyond compare. And without those French sales, we wouldn’t have been able to have the baby (who is named Christopher, and scowls all the time, as if think­ ing, like Queen Victoria, “We are not amused”). 

Do you know our dear friend Patrice Duvic? He was here just about a year ago and did some taping. My wife thinks Patrice is responsible for saving our marriage by gently quieting my aggressive violence. In any case we love him, and I talked, very seriously, about flying to Paris on my way to Poland (they are pub­ lishing UBIK in Poland, and the royalties will pay all my expenses). I knew of your PHD thesis on me, and I wish strongly to see it when you feel you’d like to show it to me. Also, I am flattered, and thank you. All that time—three years! I must say at this point, however, that you should read my Van­ couver speech, since I put in it all that my mind held for the future in case I did not live. Patrice has a copy. If you can’t get a copy (it has been published in a number of journals, such as the British _Vector,_ and in the Australian _Science Fic­ tion Quarterly)_ I will see to it that you get a copy; I will send you the U.S. print­ ing. I beg you not to conclude your thesis until you have read it. Also, Doubleday will be printing in early 1974 a new novel of mine which differs from the others, FLOW MY TEARS, THE POLICEMAN SAID. But the speech would tell you more. 

Yes, I do know of Melanie Klein’s work, especially with children. And I

have seen Calmann-Levy’s SIMUIACRAS, which is an especially lovely edition— the contrast to the original U.S. edition by Ace is appalling. I even wrote Calmann-Levy to thank them. 

We arrive now at the hard questions: “What your repetitive utilization of the simulacra theme means to you?” This is why I suggest you read my Vancou­ ver speech, because it is subtided _The Authentic Human versus the Reflex Machine_ (the tide is, “The Human and the Android”). I spent as many months writing the speech as I do writing a novel, and I tried to express in clear terms my feel­ ing that we have two types of people, one authentic, the other a cold and calculat­ ing “machine” (these are metaphors of course, “machine and android,” etc). In my speech I tried to define the authentic human and I know you will forgive me if I do not do it again here but beg off, directing you to the Vancouver speech. There are so many factors contained and expressed in the authentic human, and even the “android” or “simulacrum” are complex—the same way a color TV cir­ cuit is complex. Shortly after giving my speech in Vancouver, both at the conven­ tion and at the University of British Columbia, I decided to stay on in Canada and become a citizen. A technical qualification barred me, however, and I had to return to the U.S. But while I was there I spent a month in a heroin rehabilita­ tion center, and I saw, all around me, the “android” personality with its cold opaque eyes that reflected nothing .... I saw in the truest sense the dreadful thing I had been writing about. I think the basic issues involved (as in drug addiction) are Being versus Non-Being. Heroin suffocates the brain; it cuts down and even out all body signals. These people did not want body signals, and their eyes showed it; they did not want to live or be in any ontological sense. Freud once said that in each of us there is Eros (life) and Thanos (death). I think he was right. In many people the Eros drive wanes, and rising to fill the vacuum is the need for slow death. It is not so much that these people _want_ to die; actually they want nothing. They simply have lost the zest, the enthusiasm, the interest, for and about life; they have no curiosity, no humor ... I must admit, myself, to hav­ ing episodes when the Thanos drive begins to overpower the Eros. Several times I’ either tried to kill myself or been so depressed that had no one intervened, I would have. Is this not, then, the psychological motive for my writing about these two types? “Two hearts burn in my breast,” Goethe has Faust say, and surely this is true (I guess he said ‘Seele,’ which is soul). Yes, of course it is soul. The heart is warm and beats will forgiveness and tenderness for oneself and one’s fellow—for all man, for the animals equally. But the simulacrum person­ look into his eyes. He shows no caritas, no concern. Psychiatrically, he has been given the name “Schizoid,” which is a person who tries to think his way through life rather than feel. We, who are authentic, _feel_ and depend on those feelings. But once, long ago, when I was between 16 and 28, I was a thinking, cold, ratio­ nal, practical type. Something changed me. So I suppose in answer to your ques­ tion, I am obsessed with this question because even now, as recendy as last month, my Eros drive waned, and I considered killing myself. I do not say this to alarm you; a good therapist pulled me out. But it shows that this great fight

I have been married five times, have two girls and a new little boy. During each marriage I was the bourgeois wage-earner, and when the marriage failed I dropped (gratefully) into the gutter of near-illegal life: narcotics and guns and knives and oh so many crimes ... not so much that _I_ did them but that I surrounded myself with those who did; I embrace truly vicious people, I suppose as an antidote to the middle class safe rational spineless world my wives had forced on me. Cut loose from my children and wives I had no responsibilities to anyone but myself, and I wallowed in the gutter; and yet, to be fair to me, drew from that very gutter, lives of young people that might otherwise have been lost. I taught a young tart to enjoy James Joyce and Milton—but my real motive was to be with them, because they had a tremen­ dous vitality which made the marriages seem insipid and palid. I saw bravery far more than I saw crime, and this I caught in my work-in-progress novel, A SCAN­ NER DARKLY. That world was my world. 

It was the _stilyagi_ (the teen-age hoodlums) that I lived with and associated with, and let me say this: their integrity and courage showed how spineless and corrupt the upper middle class is. I saw more to admire in the gutter than in the tract houses, and this I can never forget. I saw them stand up to the Establish­ ment, to the police, to the average citizen, even to each other, with dignity and calm. I am only out of there because once again I am married and must lock my door each night, lest some one rip off my valuables. I was happier living with those who ripped off (i.e. stole) valuables. Eros, the force of life, is stronger in these stilyagi than in the upper middle class; Thanos can take a long and stealthy path, such as dropping in your ear the desire to watch TV every night and go no where and visit no one. I am dead serious. To me these TV watchers are suc­ cumbing to Thanos. But in the streets—do not venture out there unless your Eros is powerful enough to cause you to fight for your life. Was it Camus who said that we should live each moment with an awareness of death? He was right; when you forget death very soon death has gotten you as its victim.
