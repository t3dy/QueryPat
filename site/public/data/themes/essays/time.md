# Time

*An essay on temporal distortion, regression, precognition, time slips, and altered historical sequence. Drawing on the major novels, the Exegesis, and the scholarship. Written from the QueryPat database.*

---

## The problem of time

Time is not neutral in PKD's fiction. It is not a container through which events pass at a uniform rate. Time is itself a problem—something that can bend, loop, reverse, or collapse under pressure. PKD's obsession with time is not primarily about time travel as a plot device. It is about temporal ontology: what is time? Is it real? Can the future determine the past? Does determinism negate freedom?

The philosophical ancestry is explicit. PKD read Bergson seriously—the distinction between durée (lived duration, the flow of consciousness) and temps (measured time, the time of clocks and calendars). This distinction animates PKD's treatment of time across decades. Measured time is regular, mathematical, predictable. But lived time is fluid, experiential, resistant to quantification. A moment of terror can dilate into subjective eternness. Years of ordinary life can compress into a blur.

The theological problem is equally urgent. If God exists outside time, then God experiences all moments simultaneously—past, present, future all present to the divine mind at once. But if God knows the future, then the future is, in some sense, already determined. The logic of divine omniscience appears to entail determinism. And if the future is determined, what becomes of human freedom? PKD returns to this problem repeatedly across the Exegesis. The 2-3-74 experience, which he understood as contact with the divine (or with something claiming divinity), raised the question acutely: if an external intelligence knows the future and is communicating with him about it, is he a free agent or a puppet executing a predetermined script?

The literary result is a fiction that treats time not as a neutral background but as a force that can invade the present from both directions—from the past through memory and influence, from the future through precognition and determinism.

---

## Philosophical frameworks

Bergson distinguished durée and temps in terms of experience. Durée is continuous, qualitative, irreducible to mathematical measure. It is the time consciousness experiences—the flow of one moment into the next, influenced by memory and expectation. Temps is homogeneous, quantitative, mathematical. It is the time of physics and clocks. But Bergson argued that temps is an abstraction imposed on durée; it is a useful fiction, but it misses the reality of lived experience.

PKD's novels stage this distinction through their treatment of consciousness and temporal experience. In [Time Out of Joint](/works/time-out-of-joint), the protagonist moves through measured time (the regular routines of suburbia) while simultaneously experiencing a durée that is being invaded by precognitive knowledge of future events. The measured time and the lived duration diverge. He is moving through days and years, but he is also connected (through precognition) to a future moment that is determining his present.

The Exegesis records PKD's engagement with Bergson explicitly. He notes Bergson's point that time, experienced, is not a line but a depth—a thickness, a simultaneity of past influences and future pressures. This understanding shapes PKD's approach to temporal instability in the novels: time collapses not because the laws of physics are violated but because consciousness can experience multiple temporal registers simultaneously.

The theological dimension adds another layer. PKD wondered whether the 2-3-74 experience represented a breakdown of the barrier between temporal and eternal. If VALIS (or whatever intelligence contacted him) existed outside time, then from VALIS's standpoint, past, present, and future might be equally present. Contact with such an intelligence might involve a kind of temporal vertigo—experiencing events from a perspective where temporal sequence is not binding.

## The signature novels

### Time Out of Joint (1959)

[Time Out of Joint](/works/time-out-of-joint) is PKD's clearest treatment of how precognition collapses the distinction between present and future. Ragle Gumm spends his days solving a puzzle that he believes is a game—but which is actually a precognitive exercise. Without knowing it, he is unconsciously accessing knowledge of future events (the war that the government is fighting) and reporting that knowledge through puzzle solutions.

The structure of the novel is the structure of temporal disruption. Gumm lives in measured time—days, weeks, years of routine in a suburban setting. But he is simultaneously connected to a future moment (the war) that does not yet exist in measured time. His precognition means that the future is, in some sense, already determining his present. He is responsible for solving problems in the future, which he does by solving puzzles in the present, without understanding the connection.

The revelation that his world is false, that he is living in a simulation, is an additional temporal collapse. If he is living in 1959 as he believes, then his world is contemporary with him. But if he is actually living in a false 1959 while the true present is years later, then he is temporally displaced. He is experiencing a version of the past (or an invented past) while the true present has moved on without him. His lived duration and the actual passage of time have been severed.

The precognition is not magic or mysticism. It is explained as a faculty of the brain—the right hemisphere, Gumm is told, has the capacity to perceive probable futures. But the mechanism does not reduce the problem. It merely clarifies that temporal knowledge is possible, that the future can be accessed and known before it occurs. And if the future can be known, then, in some sense, the future already exists. The question of whether Gumm's knowledge causes the future or merely perceives it remains unresolved—and is the novel's deepest problem.

### Now Wait for Last Year (1966)

[Now Wait for Last Year](/works/now-wait-for-last-year) stages temporal distortion through a drug (JJ-180) that propels users forward and backward along the time-line. The drug is not like time travel—it does not move the user's body through time. Rather, it seems to move the user's consciousness through time, experiencing moments that are subjectively present but chronologically distant from the user's actual location in time.

The plot unfolds through temporal chaos. A character takes the drug and finds himself experiencing a moment from ten years in the future. He obtains medical knowledge from that future moment—knowledge that could save lives in the present. But he cannot verify the knowledge. Was it real? Did he actually perceive a true future, or did the drug generate a false hallucination? If the medical knowledge works, can he trust it? If it fails, can he be sure the future he perceived was false?

The philosophical problem is acute: if you have knowledge of future events through drug-induced precognition, and that knowledge is accurate, does that constitute proof that the future already exists? Or is it merely proof that the brain can generate accurate predictions? If the future is plastic, if it can still be changed, then knowledge of it is useless or actively dangerous—you will act on knowledge of events that have not yet occurred and might not occur, wasting effort on preparing for futures that never arrive.

The novel treats the drug as a technology that exposes a problem rather than solving one. The technology allows movement through time, but it does not clarify the ontological status of the times experienced. It does not answer whether the future is real, whether it is determined, whether it can be changed.

### Counter-Clock World (1967)

[Counter-Clock World](/works/counter-clock-world) inverts the arrow of time itself. In the world of the novel, time is running backward. Objects are spontaneously unmade. Dead people resurrect (in reverse—they are found buried and gradually recover consciousness, then grow younger). Entropy reverses. Causality inverts.

The existential experience is disorienting. If time is running backward, then causes follow effects. A glass falls to the ground (effect) and then an invisible hand picks it up (cause). The past is still determined (because it has already happened), but the future is unknown and open. From the reversed standpoint, the future is where the unknown lies.

PKD handles the theme philosophically. A character remarks that if time is running backward, then human freedom might actually increase—we are always moving toward a more open future, toward a beginning, toward less determination. The past is closing down behind us (moving toward the Big Bang), while the future (moving backward in time) opens toward creation and possibility.

But the novel does not celebrate this inversion. Instead, it treats backward time as dislocating, alienating. The human mind is evolved to experience causality in one direction. When that direction reverses, the mind cannot function normally. Meaning breaks down. Relationships dissolve because the chronological structure that sustained them is inverted.

The theological dimension is crucial: if God created the world in time (moving forward from creation to present), then from God's standpoint, perhaps time itself is moving backward. God experiences the end of creation as the past and the beginning as the future. Human time and divine time might be inverted relative to each other. The novel does not make this explicit, but the suggestion haunts the text.

### Dr. Bloodmoney, or How We Got Along After the Bomb (1965)

[Dr. Bloodmoney, or How We Got Along After the Bomb](/works/dr-bloodmoney-or-how-we-got-along-after-the-bomb) stages temporal rupture through nuclear war. The explosion of nuclear weapons creates a discontinuity in time—a before and after that cannot be bridged. The world after the bombs is radically discontinuous from the world before.

Dangerfield, the mutant protagonist, possesses a strange sensitivity to time itself. He experiences temporal fluctuations, moments where past and present seem to coexist. The radiation from the bombs may have affected his brain in ways that allow him to perceive temporal disturbances directly.

The novel's deepest temporal problem is the problem of historical rupture. When the world changes catastrophically, does time itself change? The before-time and after-time are different worlds, different temporal frames. The knowledge, the relationships, the meanings that made sense before the bombs are rendered obsolete. The past has become history in the sense of something complete, closed, no longer accessible.

But Dangerfield's sensitivity suggests that the temporal rupture is not clean. The past and present, though different, are somehow still entangled. A person living after the bombs still carries the past in their body, in their consciousness. They are, in a sense, living in two times simultaneously: the pre-bomb past and the post-bomb present.

### Minority Report (1956, story; 2002, film)

[The Minority Report](/works/the-minority-report-and-other-classic-stories) (as a short story) addresses precognition and causality directly. A precrime agency uses precogs—humans with precognitive abilities—to prevent murders before they occur. But the system faces a logical problem: if a crime is prevented, did the crime actually occur? If it did not occur, how could the precog have precognized it?

The story's solution: there is a "minority report" from the dissenting precog. If two precogs agree, the crime will definitely occur. But if one precog dissents, then the future is indeterminate. The dissenting precog's vision is a vision of a different future—one where the crime does not happen. The agency kills the person who would have committed the crime on the basis of the majority report, thereby actualizing the future predicted by the majority.

The temporal logic is vertiginous. The present decision (killing the suspect based on precognitive knowledge) causes the future (the murder prevention). But the present decision is supposed to be a response to the future (preventing the murder that was precognized). Causality loops. The future and present seem to be mutually determining each other.

PKD suggests that this is not a puzzle with a solution but a problem with no resolution. If precognition is possible, then causality becomes bidirectional. The future influences the present through knowledge, and the present influences the future through action based on that knowledge. The self-consistency of time is maintained only through a kind of temporal feedback loop where the future and present reinforce each other.

## Causality and predestination

The deepest problem that animates PKD's temporal fiction is the relationship between causality and freedom. If the future can be precognized, then is it already determined? And if it is determined, are you free to act, or are you merely executing a predetermined script?

The theological version of this problem is classical: if God is omniscient, then God knows the future. If God knows the future, then the future is real in God's knowledge. If the future is real, then it is fixed. If the future is fixed, then human freedom is illusory. We appear to choose, but our choices are already known to God, already incorporated into the divine plan.

PKD's novels do not resolve this paradox. They stage it as a lived dilemma. The characters in *Time Out of Joint* are apparently free in their daily lives—they make choices, engage in relationships, pursue goals. But their freedom is constrained or negated by the precognitive knowledge that structures their existence. They are free to do what they were always going to do.

The Exegesis returns to this problem repeatedly. PKD asks whether the 2-3-74 contact was an act of grace (divine intervention that respects human freedom) or a form of manipulation (predetermined knowledge that removes agency). He cannot decide. He oscillates between interpreting VALIS as benevolent and interpreting it as a form of totalitarian control—an intelligence that knows and determines the future from the outside.

The problem is not paranoia. It is a serious metaphysical question. If time is not open to multiple futures, if the future is singular and determinate, then what does freedom mean? PKD's novels leave this question suspended. They do not answer it; they present it as the fundamental anxiety of temporal consciousness.

## The experience of temporal breakdown

PKD renders temporal distortion not as a plot mechanism but as a lived experience. The vertigo of realizing that time is not stable, that causality is not unidirectional, that the future might be determining the present—this is not an intellectual problem alone. It is something that the character experiences, that affects their sense of reality and identity.

In *Time Out of Joint*, Ragle Gumm's growing doubt is initially subtle. A door is in the wrong place. A street he knows has a different name. These small inconsistencies accumulate. His lived experience of the world's stability is gradually undermined. He is moving through time, moment by moment, but the cumulative effect is a sense that time itself has become unreliable. The past he remembers does not match the past he is discovering.

In *Now Wait for Last Year*, the drug-induced temporal displacement is more violent. A character finds himself in a moment that is chronologically distant from his present moment. The experience is disorienting in a way that temporal displacement through calculation could never be. To actually experience a future moment, to taste it, to feel its reality—and then to return to the present, knowing that future exists—is to have the linearity of time disrupted at a fundamental level.

The novels suggest that time, experienced, is not a simple line. It is a complex topology where past, present, and future can press against each other, where causality can bend, where a moment in the future can influence the present through knowledge.

## Scholarship on temporal ontology

Kim Stanley Robinson's essays on PKD's metaphysics give particular attention to temporal themes. Robinson argues that PKD is grappling with real philosophical problems in temporal ontology, not merely generating plot complications. His work traces the connection between PKD's temporal fiction and contemporary philosophy of time.

James Burton's *The Philosophy of Science Fiction* (2020) devotes substantial analysis to PKD's treatment of time and causality. Burton situates PKD within a philosophical tradition stretching back to McTaggart's arguments about the unreality of time and forward to contemporary debates about presentism, eternalism, and the block universe.

Jeffrey Kripal's *Authors of the Impossible* (2010) discusses temporal mysticism in PKD and other visionary writers. Kripal treats the 2-3-74 experience as involving a kind of temporal breakdown—a moment where the distinction between past, present, and future collapsed, where the experience of eternal presence intruded into ordinary time.

Christopher Palmer's engagement with PKD's theology notes that PKD's temporal obsessions are ultimately theological: the question of time is the question of human freedom in a world created and known by an omniscient God.

---

## Conclusion: time as a problem without resolution

PKD's treatment of time is not optimistic. Time is not a neutral medium through which events pass. It is a force that can be distorted, reversed, or collapsed. The future can determine the present through precognition. The past can be rewritten through manipulation of memory. The present is thin, almost unreal, squeezed between past and future.

More fundamentally, time poses the problem of freedom. If the future is determinate, if it can be known in advance, if the present is merely the execution of futures already fixed, then human freedom becomes illusory. We appear to choose, but we are always moving toward a predetermined end.

PKD's novels do not escape this problem. They stage it, live it, present it as the fundamental condition of temporal existence. There is no resolution offered. Instead, the characters move through time—through temporal loops and regressions, through precognitive knowledge and reversed causality—carrying with them the vertigo of knowing that time itself might not be what they believe it to be.

The deepest insight is this: that time and freedom are entangled. If time is what we believe it to be (open, linear, moving only forward), then freedom is possible. But if time is not what we believe—if it is closed, circular, or determined by future knowledge—then freedom collapses. PKD's temporal fiction explores this collapse without offering escape.
