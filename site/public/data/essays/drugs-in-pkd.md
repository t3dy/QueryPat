# Drugs in Philip K. Dick

*An essay drawing on the Exegesis, the major biographies, and the corpus's drug-related vocabulary. Written from the QueryPat database.*

---

## The standing problem

The drug question follows Philip K. Dick like a court summons. Was he a hippie casualty, a clinical amphetamine addict, an authentic visionary, or all three at different hours of the same week? The literature does not converge. Lawrence Sutin's *Divine Invasions* (1989) treats the drug period as a long sad arc that PKD himself was the most lucid critic of. Anne R. Dick's *The Search for Philip K. Dick* (1995) contests Sutin on chronology and on the marriage years, insisting on a Point Reyes period (1959–1965) less dependent on amphetamines than later accounts make it. Kyle Arnold's *The Divine Madness of Philip K. Dick* (Oxford, 2016) brings clinical-psychological tools to bear and locates the drugs inside a multi-causal frame that includes the death of Jane, the November 1971 break-in, and a possible temporal-lobe substrate. Anthony Peake's *A Life of Philip K. Dick* (2013) is the most willing to entertain the visionary content as veridical, and accordingly the least interested in reducing it to pharmacology.

The portal records this disagreement rather than resolving it. The [Pamela Jackson and Jonathan Lethem](/scholars) editorial framing of the published *Exegesis* (HMH, 2011) treats the late notebooks as the most compromised possible witness — an unreliable narrator's self-account of his own unreliability — and the ontology here adopts that posture. Drug-related claims are sourced to lanes: Lane B for the *Exegesis* (PKD theorizing about himself), Lane C for the scholarship, Lane E for letters and interviews where PKD speaks under his own signature, and Lane D for synthesis biography. The five lanes appear as colored badges on the [archive](/archive). No claim made below collapses the lanes.

## What PKD said about himself

The strongest available primary text on the matter is the *Exegesis* itself, where PKD repeatedly returns to amphetamines as the ground he cannot get out from under. One representative segment reads:

> I must have gone mad (to be precise about it) sometime back in the 50s or 60s, perhaps due to the speed, then completely gone nuts around 1969 when nancy became psychotic; I was psychotic when I wrote *Tears* in '70, + certainly also in '71 + '73, + then in early '74 my psychotic 'speed'-self collapsed + a submerged sane-self invaded it.

This is Lane B speaking — PKD writing in the *Exegesis*, late 1970s, looking back. It is autobiographically ordered: speed, then breakdown, then the 1974 reorientation that he calls a sobering. The *Tears* novel (*Flow My Tears, the Policeman Said*, 1974) is offered as evidence: he was psychotic when he wrote it. He is not arguing that the novel is bad; he is dating his madness against the manuscript.

A second strain in the *Exegesis* is defensive. PKD is at pains to distinguish his February–March 1974 visionary experience from the kind of state a hallucinogen can produce. The argument is consistent across many segments and runs roughly: hallucinogens give you state (1) separated from state (2), which is normal awareness, but never state (3), which is the eternity-state he claims to have entered. Mescaline, LSD, and psilocybin are bracketed as inadequate to the phenomenon:

> In LSD states you experience (1) apart from (2); they separate without the phylogenic anamnesis necessary for (3)! to reach (3) you have to totally use up time (1) — it must be a sort of tape.

He pushes back, in the same vein, against friends and critics who suspect 2-3-74 was a flashback or a wish-fulfillment trip:

> S. took mescaline; no one got loaded. The reproach, "Phil, you experienced that because you wanted to; i.e. the swirling vines of new life" is no better an argument than, "You experienced selling *UBIK* to Doubleday because you wanted to."

Note the move: PKD treats the suspicion that he wanted the experience as logically equivalent to the suspicion that he wanted to sell *Ubik*. Both are wantings. Wanting cannot, on his account, manufacture the event. This is a Lane B argument from inside the system — the ontology cannot test it, only record that he made it.

A third strain is orthomolecular. PKD took massive vitamin C doses (he reports seven grams a day) in the period preceding 2-3-74 and credits the regimen with "improving and synchronizing neural firing." Whether vitamin overdose is a "drug" in the intended sense is a definitional question; orthomolecular psychiatry was a fashionable framework in mid-1970s northern California and PKD's adoption of it situates him precisely within that scene.

A fourth strain concerns the LSD-as-paving-stone hypothesis. PKD quotes Hofmann's theory that LSD figured in the Eleusinian Greater Mysteries and John Allegro's claim that early Christianity grew out of a psilocybin cult. In one segment he allows that earlier psychedelic experience may have "paved the way" for the activation of whatever neural circuit he then operated, while still insisting the 2-3-74 event itself was not chemical:

> When I saw the second signal, the plasmate, etc in (3-74) I was intoxicated, not by LSD — it wasn't a flash-back — but by — Camouflaged nature God — life form — Valis.

The grammatical structure is doing the work: "intoxicated, not by LSD — but by Valis." The state is granted to be intoxication; the agent is contested.

## The novels

Four of PKD's novels make drugs structural rather than incidental.

***The Three Stigmata of Palmer Eldritch*** (1965) constructs the most famous fictional drug scheme in his work: Can-D, a translation drug that places its users inside a shared simulated paradise centered on the Perky Pat dollhouse, and Chew-Z, the new drug introduced from Proxima by the returning Palmer Eldritch, which traps its users inside a paradise whose host turns out to be Eldritch himself. The metaphysics is theological; the mechanism is pharmaceutical. Can-D and Chew-Z stand in for two different ontologies of escape.

***Now Wait for Last Year*** (1966) tracks JJ-180, a drug that propels its users along the time-line, frequently into futures from which they bring back medical knowledge they then cannot use. The novel uses pharmacology to do plot work that the genre would otherwise route through technology.

***A Scanner Darkly*** (1977) is the canonical reference. The novel is dedicated to a list of friends, dead or "permanently impaired," from drug-related causes, and the dedication ends with the line that has become the standard citation for PKD's mature view: *"Drug misuse is not a disease, it is a decision, like the decision to step out in front of a moving car."* Substance D, the fictional drug at the novel's center, is widely read as a composite — methamphetamine in its compulsions, psychedelics in its splitting of consciousness, and an unspecified hallucinogen in its long-term cognitive damage. The narrative apparatus — the scramble suit, the dual identity of Bob Arctor and Officer Fred — externalizes the drug's central effect, which is the dissolution of self-recognition. The novel is read by virtually every commentator as semi-autobiographical without anyone being able to say precisely which historical drug Substance D maps onto. The portal does not adjudicate this.

***Flow My Tears, the Policeman Said*** (1974), written during the *Tears* psychosis PKD himself dates, treats KR-3 as a drug given to one character which warps the protagonist Jason Taverner out of consensus reality and into a world where he was never famous. The drug is a plot device but the affect of the novel — the pop-celebrity erasure, the police-state surveillance, the late return to Dowland's lute song as title — registers exactly the precarity PKD attributes to his own drug period.

The *Exegesis* mention of the Jeter line — "the fucked-up dopers — on Substance D — were the only ones who correctly understood the reality situation" — completes the circuit. PKD reads his own novel as having gotten the diagnostic right: that drug damage is what unmasks the world as constructed.

## The biographies

Sutin's *Divine Invasions* (Harmony, 1989; expanded Citadel Twilight, 1991) provides the standard amphetamine chronology: heavy use through the 1960s production peak (when PKD was writing two and three novels a year), tapering in the early 1970s, replaced by the orthomolecular regime in 1973–1974, and finally an effective sobriety in the late 1970s through the 1982 stroke. Sutin treats the drugs as causally entangled with the productivity rather than its enemy. The five-novels-in-1964 burst that produced *The Penultimate Truth*, *The Simulacra*, *Clans of the Alphane Moon*, *Now Wait for Last Year*, and *The Crack in Space* depended, on Sutin's reading, on Semoxydrine.

Anne Dick's *The Search* contests this. Her argument, made from the inside of the Point Reyes marriage (1959–1965), is that the heavy amphetamine years were later than Sutin places them, and that the productivity was substantially the work of a writer who could maintain a household and a marriage — not the work of an addict. The dispute is registered in the contradiction registry: the chronology of late-period drug use is one of the nine known dispute zones the portal treats as requiring contradiction-surfacing rather than silent resolution. Anne Dick's account is Lane D synthesis, but her access is closer to Lane E than Sutin's.

Arnold's *The Divine Madness* (Oxford, 2016) reframes the question entirely. Arnold's clinical training makes him the only major biographer who is an actual psychologist by trade, and his book treats the drug history as one input among several into a complex etiology that includes the death of Jane (PKD's twin, who died in infancy), the 1971 break-in (variously attributed by PKD to FBI, KGB, the Black Panthers, his own staging, or a Vancouver-based trafficking network), and a possible temporal-lobe seizure substrate. Arnold does not deny the amphetamines or downplay them; he denies them the explanatory monopoly that a popular reading might give them.

Peake's *A Life of Philip K. Dick* (Arcturus, 2013) is the most permissive about taking 2-3-74 at something close to face value. Peake is a serious anomalous-cognition researcher and his book situates PKD inside the lineage of visionary writers (Blake, Yeats, Castaneda) for whom the question "but were they actually on something?" is treated as missing the point. Peake does not argue PKD was clean; he argues that a reductive drug explanation cannot do the work the visionary content requires.

These four positions do not collapse into a single biographical account. The portal records all four.

## The fan-press tradition

Perry Kinman's *Rouzleweave* #2 (May 2002) is one of the late artifacts of the PKD fan-press tradition — Kinman edited the issue, Frank Hollander digitized it from a photocopy in 2017 — and it sits alongside the *PKDS Newsletter* (Paul Williams) and the early issues of *PKD Otaku* in a fanzine ecology that did sustained primary-source work on PKD's drug life when the academy was still treating him as a pulp anomaly. The issue's full content is held in image format and not text-extracted in this build (an OCR pass is queued); the [archive entry](/archive/rouzleweave-issue-2-may-2002) notes the document's fan-press lineage. Kinman's later work — including the music research catalog discussed in the [music essay](/essays/music-in-pkd) — represents the same tradition continuing into spreadsheet form.

## What we cannot say

The portal has no reliable record of which specific street drugs PKD used at which times, beyond the well-attested amphetamines and the occasional psychedelic. Substance D in *A Scanner Darkly* does not map onto a single historical pharmacology. The "Vancouver" episode of February 1972 — the suicide attempt at the science fiction convention, the X-Kalay rehab stay, the Heroin Reform Society speech — is documented but the contextual drug use is described by participants in incompatible ways. PKD's own letters from the period are in turn unreliable, sometimes flatly contradicting each other across weeks.

The honest statement the corpus supports is this: amphetamine use is a stable fact across roughly two decades of PKD's working life; psychedelic use is asserted by some sources and minimized by others; the visionary experiences of 1974 cannot be reduced by the available evidence to either substance. Whether this is satisfying as a conclusion depends on what kind of question one was asking.
