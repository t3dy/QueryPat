# Music in Philip K. Dick

*An essay drawing on Perry Kinman's "Music in PKD" research catalog (2,067 indexed references), the Exegesis, the major biographies, and the corpus's musical vocabulary.*

---

## A figure with a record collection

Philip K. Dick lived inside music. The Kinman catalog — the most thorough census the field has of musical reference in PKD's published and unpublished work — counts **2,067 distinct references** spread across the novels, the short stories, the *Selected Letters*, the *Exegesis*, and Anne R. Dick's memoir. Kinman, writing as a fan-researcher in the *Rouzleweave / PKD Otaku* lineage, has produced what no academic has yet produced: a complete index of every song, every composer, every record label, every radio station, and every fictional musician PKD ever named on the page.

The shape of that index tells you what kind of listener PKD was. The top six composers, by reference count, are Beethoven (150), Wagner (122), Bach (93), Mozart (50), Dowland (44), and Schubert (37). The top genre, by an enormous margin, is Classical (555 references), followed by Opera (289), Rock (228), Pop (87), Jazz (85), and Lute (54). The top fictional artist is Linda Fox of *The Divine Invasion*, the angelic balladeer constructed for that novel; she logs more references than any real person. The top real artist is Linda Ronstadt (20), whose "Heart Like a Wheel" PKD repeatedly identifies in the late *Exegesis* as a personal lifeline. KPFA Berkeley dominates the radio mentions (22). KOIF Berkeley — the Spanish-language daytime AM station whose afternoon classical block PKD briefly hosted around 1962 — appears eight times.

This is the listening of an autodidact who learned to think in classical music in the late 1940s and never moved on. The Beethoven count alone is sufficient to dispose of the popular notion that PKD's late mysticism was Eastern in derivation. He is reading Sibyls and the Pre-Socratics and Eckhart and Boehme through Beethoven, not through ragas.

## The KOIF year and the record store

Before he was a novelist with a regular paycheck, PKD worked at University Music in Berkeley (1948–1951), then at Art Music in Berkeley (1951–1952). He spent his twenties handling LPs as a retail clerk, advising customers on operatic recordings, and absorbing a depth of catalog knowledge that he would still be drawing on in 1981. The autobiographical novel *Mary and the Giant* (written 1953–1954, published 1987) preserves the record-store milieu. *In Milton Lumky Territory*, *Confessions of a Crap Artist*, and the other early mainstream novels are saturated with the working culture of small-time record retail.

The KOIF episode is briefly attested in Sutin's *Divine Invasions*. PKD hosted a classical music show on the station for a short period — Sutin places it in 1962, just before the Hugo for *The Man in the High Castle* — and the ambient memory of being a classical DJ figures across his fiction. The radio device that broadcasts compulsion in *The Game-Players of Titan*, the radio carrying voice-of-God messages in *Radio Free Albemuth*, the AM-FM split that runs through the *VALIS* trilogy — all of these are written by someone who has actually run a microphone.

## The novels

The Kinman catalog identifies the novels in which music figures most densely. The top five, ranked by reference count, are: the *Selected Letters* of 1938 (175 — partly because the letters span teenage years when PKD was constantly writing about records), *In Milton Lumky Territory* / *To The High Castle* working titles (145), *Mary and the Giant* (120), the *Exegesis* (101), and Anne Dick's *The Search for Philip K. Dick* (97 — Anne's memoir is densely musical because the marriage was lived to a soundtrack). Then come *The Bubble of Confusion* (65 — *Galactic Pot-Healer*), *The Transmigration of Timothy Archer* (62), *The Simulacra* (60), *Gather Yourselves Together* (55), *The Shifting Realities of Philip K. Dick* (53), and *Radio Free Albemuth* (50).

Three novels organize themselves around music structurally rather than incidentally:

***Flow My Tears, the Policeman Said*** (1974) takes its title from John Dowland's lute song "Flow My Tears" (1600), one of the most-recorded English songs of the early baroque. The Dowland reference does theological work in the novel: the song is a Renaissance lament for lost love and lost favor, and PKD's appropriation of it for a story about pop celebrity erased into nonexistence transposes the Renaissance courtier's complaint into a 1980s dystopia. The Kinman catalog logs Dowland 44 times across the corpus — a number that registers how much weight PKD put on this single composer.

***The Divine Invasion*** (1981) builds its emotional spine around Linda Fox, the angelic singer whose "Yet you walk by my side" (composed by Dowland's "Yet I bear" in PKD's borrowing) is the protagonist Asher's repeated recourse. PKD records in the *Exegesis* that Linda Fox is modeled on Linda Ronstadt; in the same passages he identifies "Heart Like a Wheel" (Ronstadt's 1974 album and title song, written by Anna McGarrigle) as having reached him in the late 1970s as personal address. The reference cluster reads:

> The medical info about Chrissy had to do with a Beatles record. Then *Tears* (no, this was prior). Then now: Asher (*Divine Invasion* + *Heart Like a Wheel* i.e. Ronstadt).

Asher, *Divine Invasion*, and Ronstadt occupy the same theological slot in PKD's late autobiography. Music is the medium through which the divine continues to address him after the *Exegesis* notebooks have run on for years.

***VALIS*** (1981) is the novel that thematizes music most explicitly. The "Information Sickness" episode in which Horselover Fat receives transmission from the orbital satellite occurs against a background of liturgical music. The KW orchestra, the *Parsifal* references, the recurring use of Beethoven's late string quartets as figures for unconsoled human suffering — all of these are the surface of a novel in which music is one of the principal instruments by which VALIS communicates.

The Kinman catalog also flags the music sequence in *The Simulacra* (60 references) — the Richard Kongrosian subplot, in which a psychokinetic concert pianist whose performances are losing physical substance functions as a figure for an artistic culture eating itself — and the Loony Luke / Carlton B. Tweany cluster in *Gather Yourselves Together* and the early mainstream novels. These are cases in which music is a vehicle for plot and characterization rather than a metaphysical medium, but they remain saturated.

## Beethoven in the *Exegesis*

The 150 Beethoven references across the corpus cluster heavily in the late *Exegesis*. The portal's [Beethoven dictionary entry](/dictionary) records 59 mentions in the *Exegesis* alone, with peak density in the September–October 1976 segments and a second peak in the Pat folder of April 1981. The references are not biographical (PKD is not writing music history) but figural. Beethoven is the exemplary case of human freedom — the figure who, by composing his way through deafness and isolation, demonstrates the possibility of evolving consciousness against material constraint. PKD writes:

> Beethoven's contribution to what we conceive of as the person being the good example; the key term is freedom.

This is Beethoven as Hegel's aesthetic exemplar, transposed into a 1976 northern California idiom. PKD has been reading Hegel's *Lectures on the Philosophy of History* in the same months and the *Exegesis* explicitly couples the Beethoven figure with Hegel's "freedom realizing itself in history."

A second, darker Beethoven appears as well. PKD invokes the composer as exemplar of *astral determinism* — the fate the artist cannot escape — and credits Christ's intervention with breaking that fate in his own case:

> Only Christ's intervention saved me from reliving the fate of Beethoven. My lucid awareness of astral-determinism dooming me would explain the anxiety growing from 1971 to its peak in early 1974 — whereupon Christ broke that fate, + my anxiety departed.

The portal does not collapse this contradiction. Beethoven is both the figure of human freedom and the figure of fated suffering, and the *Exegesis* uses both functions at different points without ever resolving them. This is not editorial sloppiness; it is the way the late notebooks work. Beethoven, like Christ, like the apostle Thomas, like the Tractate Cryptica Scriptura's figure of the divine logos, is plural in PKD's hands.

## The "music-color-math" triune

The most peculiar musical content in the corpus is PKD's account of the 2-3-74 visions themselves. The visions, on his repeated description, were not auditory in the ordinary sense — he was not hearing music — but they registered as triune information of a kind for which "music" was one of three names:

> The color, musical score, math triune info: like an illuminated manuscript from the Medieval period.

And:

> They did transmit the self-authenticating "music-color-math" annotation expressing a concept that can't arise within a human mind.

The "music-color-math triune" is PKD's term for the structure of the transmission: a single information packet legible simultaneously as music, color, and mathematics. The illuminated-manuscript analogy locates the experience within a Western mystical-aesthetic tradition (Hildegard of Bingen, the Rothschild Canticles, the medieval *trivium*-as-cosmology) rather than within any drug-state or hypnagogic phenomenology. Whether this is convincing is for the reader to judge; that PKD held to the triune description across years of self-revisions is a stable finding of the corpus.

## What music is for, in PKD

The Kinman catalog and the *Exegesis* together make available a coherent if non-systematic account of what music does in PKD's work:

1. **Music is anamnesis.** It returns the listener to a state — usually identified with childhood, sometimes with a pre-natal or pre-incarnate "true" condition — that ordinary perception covers over. The Dowland title of *Flow My Tears* and the Linda Fox / Ronstadt cluster of *The Divine Invasion* both perform this function.

2. **Music is divine transmission.** The 2-3-74 vision is "music-color-math triune info." VALIS's communications, in the late novels, are characteristically musical in form. This function is theological, not aesthetic.

3. **Music is exemplum of freedom.** Beethoven, in the late *Exegesis*, is the pattern of human consciousness evolving against constraint. The composer figure is for PKD what Christ is in another register: a sign that personhood is not closed.

4. **Music is the trap of artifice.** The opposite function is also present. In *The Three Stigmata of Palmer Eldritch*, the simulated paradises Can-D users enter are scored. In *VALIS*, the false revelations sometimes arrive musically. In *The Simulacra*, Kongrosian's failing concerts mark a culture that has confused performance with reality. The same medium can be either anamnesis or trap; the corpus does not provide a rule for distinguishing them.

5. **Music is biographical infrastructure.** PKD wrote inside it. Anne Dick's memoir documents the Point Reyes years as lived to a soundtrack of Wagner and Beethoven on a high-quality turntable. Sutin's biography records the late 1970s Santa Ana years as accompanied by Linda Ronstadt and the Beatles. The character of "writing" as a daily practice was for PKD a thing one did in a room with records playing.

## Erik Davis on Sonic Youth

A final note belongs to the late reception of PKD's music life. [Erik Davis](/scholars)'s *SPIN* magazine piece (January 1989), now [archived in the corpus](/archive), records Thurston Moore of Sonic Youth identifying *VALIS* as a direct influence on the band's 1987 album *Sister*. The album is named for PKD's stillborn twin sister Jane, whose death PKD treated across the *Exegesis* as the foundational trauma. Sonic Youth's "Schizophrenia" — the opening track — is a closer reading of PKD's twin obsession than most academic articles. The portal records the connection because reception is part of the corpus. Music in PKD points outward as well as inward.
